SQL or NoSQL — And the Six Moves Before You Choose

"Postgres won't scale" is the most expensive sentence in system design, and it's almost always said before anyone has run EXPLAIN. Here's what the relational model actually buys you, the six techniques that take it much further than people think — tuning, denormalization, replication, federation, sharding — and how to tell when you've genuinely outgrown it.

01 The Question Behind "Which Database?"

Someone opens a design doc with "we're using MongoDB." Someone else replies "why not Postgres?" Ninety minutes disappear. Nobody in the room has said a single sentence about what the data looks like or how it will be read.

That's the shape of almost every database argument I've sat through, and it's backwards. The technology is the last decision, not the first. What comes before it:

  1. What are the access patterns? Which queries run a thousand times a second, and which run once a day?
  2. What's the read/write ratio? Most systems read 100–1000× more than they write. Yours might not.
  3. What has to be transactional? Which operations genuinely cannot be partially applied?
  4. How much data, and how fast is it growing? Gigabytes and terabytes are different problems.
  5. What's the shape of the data? Highly relational, document-like, or just a very large hash table?

Answer those five and the technology choice usually makes itself. Skip them and you'll pick something fashionable, then spend two years discovering which of its trade-offs you can't live with.

Access patterns are the input; the database is the output. Any conversation that starts with a product name has skipped the part that determines the answer.

💡 The One-Line Version

Start relational, learn its six scaling moves properly, and reach for NoSQL when a specific access pattern genuinely doesn't fit — not when the row count gets big. A well-tuned Postgres handles far more than most teams believe, and it will hand you joins and transactions for free while it does.

02 What the Relational Model Actually Buys You

A relational database stores data in tables with a declared schema and lets you combine them at query time. That's the surface. Underneath, you're paying for four things that are genuinely hard to build yourself, and it's worth knowing exactly what they are before you give them up.

🔒

ACID Transactions

All-or-nothing writes across multiple rows and tables. The hardest thing on this list to replicate in application code.

🔗

Joins

Combine data at read time. Store each fact once and assemble any shape you need later.

📐

Schema & Constraints

The database refuses invalid data. A guarantee, not a convention someone can forget.

🧠

A Query Planner

You declare what you want; decades of optimizer research decides how. Nobody hand-writes access paths.

ACID, Concretely

  • Atomicity — a transaction either fully happens or fully doesn't. Debit and credit land together or neither lands.
  • Consistency — every committed transaction leaves the database satisfying its declared rules: foreign keys, unique constraints, check constraints. (Note this is a different "consistency" from the CAP kind — see the availability primer.)
  • Isolation — concurrent transactions don't see each other's half-finished work. The isolation level decides how strictly.
  • Durability — once committed, it survives a crash. The write-ahead log is on disk before the commit returns.

The reason this matters for the SQL-versus-NoSQL question: if you drop ACID, you don't stop needing it. You just move it into your application, where you'll implement a worse version with sagas, compensating transactions, and idempotency keys. Sometimes that's the correct trade. It is never a free one.

💡 The Underrated Feature Is the Planner

In SQL you write what you want. The planner examines statistics about your data, considers index scans, sequential scans, hash joins, merge joins, and nested loops, estimates the cost of each, and picks a plan. When the data distribution changes, it picks differently — with no code change from you.

In most NoSQL stores you write how to get it, and the access path is baked into the schema. That's exactly why NoSQL is fast when your queries match the design and painful when a new query doesn't.

The six sections that follow are the standard toolkit for pushing a relational database much further than the "it doesn't scale" reflex assumes. They're roughly in order of cost — start at the top.

03 Move 1 — SQL Tuning, the Cheapest Scaling There Is

Before any architecture changes: most databases described as "at their limit" are running a handful of queries doing far more work than necessary. Tuning is an afternoon. Sharding is a quarter. Do the afternoon first.

Benchmark, Then Profile — In That Order

Benchmark to reproduce load: a tool like ab, wrk, or k6 pointed at a realistic mix of requests against a realistic volume of data. A benchmark on a 1,000-row table tells you nothing about a 100-million-row table, because the planner will genuinely choose a different strategy.

Profile to find the culprits: turn on the slow query log, and sort by total time consumed rather than by worst single execution. A 40ms query that runs 10,000 times a minute is a far bigger problem than a 4-second report that runs hourly, and only the totals column shows you that.

Indexes — What They Are and What They Cost

An index is a separate, sorted structure — almost always a B-tree — that maps column values to row locations. Sorted order turns a full scan into a descent through a shallow tree: logarithmic instead of linear. On a 10-million-row table that's the difference between examining ten million rows and examining roughly two dozen.

Diagram 2 — Same Query, Two Plans

3,000× fewer rows examined for one CREATE INDEX. This is why tuning comes before every architectural move — nothing else on the list has that ratio.

Index the columns you filter, join, group, and sort on. But every index is a write tax: each INSERT has to update every index on the table. A table with nine indexes has slow writes, and some of those indexes are almost certainly unused — every major database exposes index usage statistics, and reviewing them is a ten-minute job that frequently finds several to drop.

SQL — Read the Plan Before You Guess 
-- Never optimize by intuition. Ask the planner what it's doing.
EXPLAIN ANALYZE
SELECT o.id, o.total, c.name
FROM   orders o
JOIN   customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
WHERE  o.created_at >= '2026-08-01'
  AND  o.status = 'PENDING'
ORDER  BY o.created_at DESC
LIMIT  50;

-- Things to look for in the output:
--   "Seq Scan" on a big table        → a missing index
--   rows estimated ≫ rows actual     → stale statistics; ANALYZE
--   "Sort" on a large set            → index the ORDER BY column
--   nested loop over many rows       → the join order is wrong

-- A composite index matching filter + sort order serves all three:
CREATE INDEX idx_orders_status_created
    ON orders (status, created_at DESC)
    INCLUDE (total, customer_id);   -- covering: no table lookup at all
💡 Column Order in a Composite Index Is Not Cosmetic

An index on (status, created_at) can serve queries filtering on status, or on status AND created_at — but not one filtering on created_at alone. The leftmost columns must be present. Think of it as a phone book sorted by surname then first name: brilliant for finding "Kotnala, A", useless for finding everyone named Ajay.

Rule of thumb: equality filters first, then range filters, then sort columns.

The N+1 Query — Still the Most Common Bug in Production

Fetch 100 orders, then loop and fetch each order's customer. That's 101 round trips where one would do. Each is fast in isolation, which is exactly why it survives code review and profiling at low volume, and why it destroys throughput at high volume — 101× the round trips, 101× the connection pool pressure.

Java — The Same Work, 101 Trips vs 1 
// ✕ N+1: reads fine, behaves terribly
List<Order> orders = orderRepo.findRecent(100);       // 1 query
for (Order o : orders) {
    o.setCustomer(customerRepo.findById(o.customerId())); // 100 more
}

// ✓ One round trip — let the database do the join
List<OrderWithCustomer> rows = orderRepo.findRecentWithCustomers(100);

// ✓ Or two, if a join is awkward — batch the second fetch
List<Order> orders = orderRepo.findRecent(100);
Set<Long> ids  = orders.stream().map(Order::customerId).collect(toSet());
Map<Long, Customer> byId = customerRepo.findAllById(ids)   // 1 query, IN (...)
        .stream().collect(toMap(Customer::id, identity()));
orders.forEach(o -> o.setCustomer(byId.get(o.customerId())));

Schema Choices That Compound

  • Use the narrowest type that fits. Rows are read in fixed-size disk pages, so narrower rows mean more rows per page, which means fewer page reads and a much better chance the working set stays in memory. Bloated column types are a quiet, permanent tax on every query.
  • Fixed-width where it's genuinely fixed. A country code is always two characters; CHAR(2) lets the engine compute offsets directly instead of scanning for the end of a variable-length value.
  • DECIMAL for money, never a float. Binary floating point cannot represent 0.10 exactly. Financial totals accumulate error, and the resulting bug reports are extremely unfun.
  • Don't store large blobs in the row. Put the image in object storage and the URL in the column. A megabyte inline destroys page density for every query that touches the table, including ones that don't select the blob.
  • NOT NULL wherever it's true. Nullable columns cost a bit of per-row overhead and, more importantly, make the planner's job harder and your queries subtler.
  • Bulk-load with indexes off. Loading ten million rows into an indexed table updates every index ten million times. Drop the indexes, load, rebuild — often an order of magnitude faster.
⚠️ Measure, Don't Fold in Folklore

Every one of these has a "but". Query caches sound obviously good and have been removed from modern MySQL because their invalidation lock became a bottleneck under concurrency. Covering indexes are excellent until the extra columns make the index too large to stay in memory. CHAR versus VARCHAR matters far less on a modern SSD than it did on spinning disks.

The permanent advice isn't a list of settings — it's EXPLAIN ANALYZE on your data, at your volume, with your query mix.

Table partitioning — sharding's much easier cousin

+

Partitioning splits one logical table into several physical pieces inside the same database — typically by date range, so each month lives in its own partition. The application still sees one table and writes ordinary SQL; the planner prunes irrelevant partitions automatically.

The wins are real and cheap. Queries scoped to recent data touch only the recent partitions, so the hot set stays in memory. Deleting old data becomes dropping a partition — instant, rather than a DELETE that churns through millions of rows and bloats the table. Index maintenance is per-partition, so bulk loads stay fast.

It's worth being precise about what it doesn't do: partitioning is still one server, so it doesn't add write capacity or survive that server's failure. But when the problem is "this table is too big to keep hot" rather than "this machine can't keep up", partitioning solves it for a fraction of sharding's cost — and it's reversible.

04 Move 2 — Denormalization: Trading Writes for Reads

Normalization stores every fact exactly once. It's the right default: no contradictions possible, no update anomalies, minimal storage. The cost is that answering a real question means reassembling those facts with joins, and joins over large tables get expensive.

Denormalization deliberately breaks that rule. You store redundant copies of data so a common read doesn't have to join. Reads get faster and simpler; writes get slower and more dangerous, because now the same fact lives in several places and they can disagree.

The reason this trade usually pays: most systems read far more than they write — commonly 100:1, often 1000:1. Making a write 20% more expensive to make a read 10× cheaper is, arithmetically, an excellent deal.

Diagram 3 — Four Joins, or One Row

Denormalization moves work from read time to write time. That's only a win when reads dominate — which they usually do, but check rather than assume.

Materialized Views — Denormalization the Database Maintains

Hand-maintained duplicate columns are where denormalization goes wrong: someone adds a new write path and forgets to update the copy. A materialized view avoids that by letting the database own the duplication — you declare the query, it stores the result, and you refresh it on a schedule or on demand.

SQL — Let the Database Maintain the Copy 
-- The expensive aggregate, computed once instead of per request
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW customer_order_stats AS
SELECT c.id           AS customer_id,
       c.name,
       COUNT(o.id)    AS order_count,
       SUM(o.total)   AS lifetime_value,
       MAX(o.created_at) AS last_order_at
FROM   customers c
LEFT   JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
GROUP  BY c.id, c.name;

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ON customer_order_stats (customer_id);

-- Refresh without blocking readers (Postgres):
REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY customer_order_stats;

-- The trade is explicit and visible: this data is as fresh as
-- your last refresh. That's a staleness budget you chose,
-- not one you discovered during an incident.
⚠️ Denormalize Late, and Write Down Why

Normalize first. Denormalize only when a specific query is measurably too slow and you've already indexed it properly. Premature denormalization gives you all of the write complexity for none of the read benefit, and it's much harder to undo than to add.

When you do it, leave a comment naming the source of truth and the query it exists for. Six months later, the person deciding whether a copy can be dropped will be someone else.

05 Move 3 — Master-Slave Replication

One database accepts writes. Its changes stream to one or more read-only copies. Reads spread across all of them; writes all land on the one primary.

This is the first genuinely architectural move on the list, and it's a big one — for a read-heavy workload, which is most of them, it multiplies your read capacity by the number of replicas for the cost of running them. It's also usually a configuration change plus a routing change in the application, not a rewrite.

Diagram 4 — Primary and Replicas: Reads Scale, Writes Don't

Replication lag is per-replica, and the worst replica defines the worst user experience. An average lag of 8ms hides the node that's 40 seconds behind and serving a slice of your traffic.

What You Get and What It Costs

Upside Downside
Read capacity scales with replica count Write capacity is unchanged — one primary, forever
Replicas double as hot standbys for failover Promotion is a procedure that can go wrong, and rarely gets rehearsed
Analytics can run on a replica without hurting production Async replication means a failover can lose recently acknowledged writes
Usually config plus read/write routing, not a rewrite Every replica applies every write — replication itself is load

That last point catches people out. A replica isn't free capacity: it does all the write work the primary does, plus serves reads. Adding replicas to a write-bound system adds cost and no capacity at all.

💡 Sync, Semi-Sync, Async — One Knob, Big Consequences

Async (the default almost everywhere): the primary acknowledges as soon as its own disk write lands. Fastest writes; a failover loses whatever hadn't shipped yet. Semi-synchronous: acknowledge once at least one replica confirms receipt. One extra round trip, and now your data-loss window is bounded rather than unknown. Synchronous: wait for all (or a quorum of) replicas. Zero loss, and your write latency is now the slowest replica's latency.

Semi-sync is the pragmatic middle for most systems, and the fact that it's rarely the default is worth knowing.

06 Move 4 — Master-Master Replication

Both nodes accept writes and replicate to each other. On paper it fixes the single-writer ceiling and removes the promotion step from failover entirely. In practice it introduces a problem that has no clean general solution: two nodes can modify the same row at the same time, and something has to decide who wins.

Diagram 5 — Two Writers, One Row, No Referee

Master-master is safe when the two writers never touch the same rows. Once they can, you're running a distributed conflict-resolution problem, and last-write-wins is a decision to lose data quietly.

Where It Genuinely Fits

  • Geographically partitioned writes. EU customers write to the EU primary, APAC to the APAC primary, and the two datasets barely overlap. Latency improves for everyone, data residency gets easier, and conflicts are rare by construction.
  • High-availability pairs where one side is effectively idle. Both are configured as primaries so failover is instant, but the load balancer sends all traffic to one at a time.
  • Append-mostly workloads where records are written once and rarely updated — event logs, audit trails — so there's nothing to conflict over.

Where it does not fit: a globally contended dataset. If any row can be updated from any region, you have signed up for conflict resolution on your hottest keys, and you'll be building it yourself.

⚠️ Two Traps Specific to Master-Master

Auto-increment collisions. Two primaries independently assigning sequential IDs will hand out the same number. The usual fixes are offset/increment settings (A uses odd, B uses even) or switching to UUIDs — but decide before launch, not after two rows share an ID.

It doesn't double your write throughput. Every node still applies every write — its own plus everything replicated in. Two primaries do not give you 2× write capacity; they give you write locality and faster failover. Only partitioning the data (§08) adds write capacity.

07 Move 5 — Federation: Splitting by Function

Federation — functional partitioning — splits one database into several, divided by business domain rather than by rows. Users in one database, products in another, orders in a third. Each has its own hardware, its own primary, and its own write path.

The wins compound in a way that's easy to underestimate:

  • Writes go parallel. Three primaries instead of one means three independent write paths. This is the first move on the list that actually raises write capacity.
  • Each database gets smaller, so a larger fraction of each working set fits in memory. Cache hit rates rise, and disk reads fall — often the single biggest practical effect.
  • Blast radius shrinks. The analytics database melting down no longer takes checkout with it.
  • Independent tuning and scaling. The write-heavy events database and the read-heavy catalogue can have completely different index strategies, hardware, and replica counts.
Diagram 6 — Federation: Three Databases, Three Write Paths, One Broken Join

Federation is the same idea as splitting a monolith into services, applied to storage — and it brings the same benefit and the same bill: independence, minus the joins and transactions that used to cross the line.

⚠️ Draw the Line Where the Queries Aren't

Federation works when the domains are genuinely loosely coupled. It fails when your most important query spans the split — you've turned one indexed join into two round trips plus an in-memory merge, on the hot path.

Before splitting, list your top ten queries and check how many cross the proposed boundary. If it's more than one or two, the boundary is in the wrong place. And note the transaction loss: "create user and their first order atomically" is no longer a thing the database can do for you.

08 Move 6 — Sharding: Splitting by Data

Sharding splits a single logical table across multiple databases, each holding a subset of the rows. Same schema everywhere; users A–H on shard 1, I–P on shard 2, and so on. It's the only technique on this list with no ceiling — and the only one you'll still be living with in five years.

Choosing How to Split

#️⃣

Hash

Hash the key, mod the shard count. Even distribution, no range queries, painful to resize without consistent hashing.

📏

Range

Contiguous key ranges per shard. Range scans work beautifully; sequential keys create a permanent hot shard.

📖

Directory

A lookup service maps key to shard. Total flexibility, easy rebalancing, and a new component on every request path.

🌍

Geographic

Split by region. Great for latency and data residency; badly skewed if your users are.

Diagram 7 — The Shard Key Decides Everything

Sharding is the only move here that is genuinely hard to reverse. The key deserves a week of argument before it gets a day of implementation.

Consistent Hashing — Why Plain Modulo Hurts

The naive scheme is shard = hash(key) % N. It distributes evenly and it's trivially simple. It also means that changing N changes the destination of nearly every key: going from 4 shards to 5 remaps roughly 80% of your data, which is a migration of the entire dataset just to add one machine.

Consistent hashing places shards and keys on a ring, and each key belongs to the next shard clockwise. Add a shard and only the keys in one arc move — roughly 1/N of the data instead of nearly all of it. Virtual nodes (many ring positions per physical shard) smooth out the distribution.

The Resize Cost, Concretely 
MODULO:  4 shards → 5 shards
  key 1001: 1001 % 4 = 1  →  1001 % 5 = 1   stays
  key 1002: 1002 % 4 = 2  →  1002 % 5 = 2   stays
  key 1003: 1003 % 4 = 3  →  1003 % 5 = 3   stays
  key 1004: 1004 % 4 = 0  →  1004 % 5 = 4   MOVES
  key 1005: 1005 % 4 = 1  →  1005 % 5 = 0   MOVES
  …roughly 80% of all keys move. Full dataset migration.

CONSISTENT HASHING:  4 shards → 5 shards
  only the keys in the new node's arc move
  …roughly 20% of keys move (1/N). The rest never notice.
⚠️ The Celebrity Problem Has No Elegant Fix

Shard by user_id and your distribution is perfect — until one account has 200 million followers and every read of that account hits one shard. The unit of distribution is indivisible: you cannot split one user across two shards without abandoning the scheme.

The real answers are all workarounds, and you pick one deliberately: cache the hot entity aggressively so most reads never reach the shard; give known whales their own dedicated shard; or add a synthetic suffix to the key for the hottest entities and fan out reads across the resulting sub-keys. None of them are free, and all of them are easier to add before launch than during an incident.

09 The Ladder — What Order to Do All This In

Six techniques, wildly different costs. The order matters more than any individual choice, because each rung buys you time to do the next one properly instead of at 2 a.m.

Diagram 8 — The Scaling Ladder: Cheap and Reversible First

Each rung buys time. The value of that time is what makes the order matter: step 1 done well often means step 6 never happens.

💡 Caching Deserves Its Rung

Notice step 2 — it isn't one of the six database techniques, and it's frequently the single highest-leverage move available. A cache in front of a read-heavy database removes most of the load without changing the schema, the topology, or anything about the write path.

It comes with its own failure modes — thundering herds, hot keys, staleness — which is why they get their own deep dive. But on a cost-per-unit-of-relief basis, nothing above it competes.

10 NoSQL — What the "No" Actually Means

"NoSQL" is a terrible name for a real idea. It isn't about disliking a query language — several NoSQL stores ship SQL-like dialects. The name is usually back-fitted as "Not Only SQL," which is closer.

What actually unites the category is a set of deliberate removals. NoSQL stores drop features of the relational model in exchange for things the relational model finds hard: horizontal write scaling, schema flexibility, and predictable performance at very large volumes.

What's given up What's bought with it
Joins — data is denormalized, assembly happens in your code Rows can live on any node; no cross-node join to coordinate
Multi-row ACID transactions (mostly; some now offer limited ones) No distributed locking or two-phase commit on the write path
A rigid enforced schema Fields vary per record; no migration to add one
Ad-hoc querying — you query the way you designed for Predictable, often single-digit-millisecond access at any scale
Strong consistency by default Availability during partitions; writes accepted anywhere

BASE, the Counterpart to ACID

The philosophy is usually summarised as BASE — Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency. Read plainly: the system stays up and answers, data may be in flux, and replicas converge given time. It's the same trade covered in the availability vs consistency primer, viewed from the storage layer.

⚠️ Two Myths Worth Retiring

"NoSQL means no schema." It means no enforced schema. The schema still exists — it now lives in your application code, in every place that reads a document and assumes a field is there. That's not less schema; it's unversioned, unchecked schema scattered across a codebase. Records written in 2023 will still be in the collection in 2027, in the old shape.

"NoSQL is faster." Not inherently. It's faster for the access pattern it was designed around, and often dramatically slower for one it wasn't. A relational database with the right index will beat a document store doing a collection scan every time.

11 The Four NoSQL Families

Treating NoSQL as one thing is the root of most bad choices in this area. There are four families with genuinely different data models, and picking between them matters far more than picking between two products in the same family.

Diagram 9 — Four Data Models, Drawn as the Data Actually Looks

The boundaries blur in practice — DynamoDB is both key-value and document; several key-value stores index into their values. The distinction that matters is what the store lets you ask for cheaply.

Key-Value Store

A hash table, distributed. Put a value under a key, get it back by that key, in O(1). No querying by content, no relationships, no partial reads of a value — the key is the only way in.

That severity is the point. With no query planner, no join logic, and no secondary index to maintain, a key-value store does one thing at extreme speed and scales horizontally almost trivially: any node can own any key range. Many keep keys in sorted order, which quietly gives you efficient range scans over a key prefix — genuinely useful for time-ordered data.

The complexity doesn't disappear; it moves into your application, which now owns anything resembling a relationship or a secondary lookup. Best for: sessions, shopping carts, rate-limit counters, feature flags, caching layers, leaderboards.

Document Store

Values become structured documents — JSON, BSON, XML — and the store can look inside them. You can query on nested fields, build secondary indexes, and update part of a document without rewriting it.

The natural fit is data that is genuinely tree-shaped and read as a unit. A product with variants, images, and attributes; a user with addresses and preferences. In a relational model that's five tables and four joins; as a document it's one read of one contiguous record.

The flexible schema is the double-edged part. Adding a field costs nothing — no migration, no downtime. But documents written years apart coexist in the same collection with different shapes, and every reader must cope. Best for: catalogues, user profiles, content management, event payloads, anything whose shape varies by record.

Wide Column Store

The most misunderstood family, largely because "column" suggests a spreadsheet. The model is closer to a nested map: a row key points to a set of column families, and each family holds arbitrarily many columns. Crucially, each row can have entirely different columns, and empty columns cost nothing at all — a row with three columns and a row with three million sit happily side by side.

Row keys are kept in sorted order, which makes range scans over a key span cheap, and every cell carries a timestamp, which gives you versioning and a conflict-resolution mechanism for free. That combination is why the family dominates time-series and event data: make the row key (device_id, day) and the columns timestamps, and "give me every reading from this sensor last Tuesday" becomes one sequential read.

These systems — Bigtable and its descendants HBase and Cassandra — are built for very large datasets and very high write rates, with linear horizontal scaling. The cost is that queries must match the key design; an unanticipated query pattern can mean rewriting how the data is stored. Best for: time series, IoT telemetry, logs, messaging history, tables measured in terabytes and up.

Graph Database

Nodes are records, edges are relationships, and both can carry properties. The distinguishing feature is that traversing an edge is a pointer hop rather than an index lookup — so the cost of walking a relationship doesn't grow with the size of the database.

That's the whole value proposition, and it only matters for queries that walk many hops. "Friends of friends who bought this" is three self-joins in SQL, and each join multiplies the intermediate result set; at six degrees it's unrunnable. As a traversal, it's proportional to the number of edges actually visited.

The trade-offs are real: a smaller ecosystem, fewer engineers who know it, and horizontal scaling that's genuinely hard, because a graph resists partitioning — any cut you make severs edges that queries then have to cross. Best for: social graphs, fraud-ring detection, recommendation engines, permission and org hierarchies, network topology.

🌿 Don't Reach for a Graph Database Too Early

Having relationships in your data is not a reason to use a graph database — relational databases are named after relations. The reason is deep traversal: queries that follow chains three, four, six hops out, where each hop would be another join.

A social network's "people you may know" is a genuine case. A blog with authors and tags is not. Two joins are not a graph problem.

12 SQL or NoSQL — Choosing by Access Pattern

Now the actual question. The honest answer is that this is rarely a fork in the road and usually a question asked once per dataset — but there are real signals in each direction.

Diagram 10 — The Decision, Driven by the Data

Data volume is the weakest signal on this chart, and the one most often used to justify a switch. Access pattern and data shape are what actually decide.

Polyglot Persistence — and Its Bill

Using several stores, each matched to a workload, is the mature answer. It's also how you end up with five databases, five backup strategies, five failure modes, five on-call runbooks, and a data consistency story that spans all of them.

The tempering rule: every additional datastore should earn its place against a specific, measured problem. "It seemed like a better fit" is not a measurement. And note that a modern relational database has quietly absorbed a lot of the competition — Postgres has excellent JSONB support with indexing, full-text search, array and range types, and range partitioning. For a great many workloads, "use Postgres for that too" is genuinely the right call and one fewer system to operate.

💡 The Question That Cuts Through It

"What query does this store make easy that our current one makes hard — and how often do we run it?"

If there's a crisp answer, the new store is probably justified. If the answer is about scale in the abstract, or about how modern something feels, you're about to buy an operational burden without a matching benefit.

If you do migrate: how to do it without a big-bang cutover

+

Dual-write, then verify, then flip. Write to both the old and new store for a period while continuing to read from the old one. Backfill history in the background. Run a continuous comparison job that reads the same keys from both and reports mismatches — that job is the whole point, and it's the step people skip.

Move reads gradually. Shift a small percentage of read traffic to the new store behind a flag, compare results, then ramp. Keep the old store writable and current until you're confident, because the ability to flip back within seconds is what makes the migration safe.

Expect the dual-write window to be long — months, not days, for anything important — and budget for the fact that dual writes can themselves diverge when one side fails. An idempotent write path and a reconciliation job aren't optional extras here; they're the mechanism.

13 Five Ways This Goes Wrong in Production

The same material as incidents. Each one is a team making a defensible decision and meeting the part of it they hadn't priced in.

1. The Migration That Was a Missing Index

Queries slow down as the table crosses 50 million rows. The team concludes the relational model has hit its limit and spends a quarter moving to a document store. Latency improves — because the new store was designed around the one access pattern that mattered, and it got a fresh index on the right field. The same index on the original table would have delivered the same result in an afternoon. They now own a second database, no joins, and no transactions. Always run EXPLAIN before you run a migration.

2. Read Replicas Added to a Write-Bound System

Write latency climbs. Someone adds three read replicas. Nothing improves, and the primary gets marginally slower because it's now also feeding three replication streams. Replicas scale reads; writes still go through one node. The tell is the ratio — check writes per second against reads per second before choosing a scaling move, because the two problems look identical on a latency graph and have opposite fixes.

3. Sharded on Tenant ID, Then Signed an Enterprise Customer

The distribution is beautiful across four thousand small tenants. Then one customer arrives representing 40% of all data and traffic, permanently pinned to one shard. Adding shards doesn't help — a tenant is indivisible. The team ends up building a dedicated shard for that customer and special routing for it, which is a reasonable answer that would have been much cheaper to design in from the start. Any shard key with a natural whale needs an escape hatch on day one.

4. "Schemaless" Meant Five Schemas

Two years into a document store, one collection contains records written by four generations of the application. Some have address as a string, some as an object, some as an array of objects, and some not at all. Every read path is defensive; every new feature starts with an archaeology session. The schema never went away — it stopped being enforced, versioned, or visible. Schema validation and an explicit version field on every document cost almost nothing at the start and are close to unaffordable to retrofit.

5. Eventual Consistency Met a Business Rule

Inventory moves to an eventually consistent store for write throughput. It works, until two customers buy the last unit within the replication window and both get confirmations. The store did exactly what it promised; nobody had asked whether "briefly wrong" was acceptable for this particular number. Some data tolerates staleness and some doesn't, and the boundary is a business decision — not one the database can make for you. This is the per-operation choice arriving at the storage layer.

⚠️ The Common Thread

Four of these five are the same mistake: a technology was chosen to fix a symptom before anyone measured the cause. The database was rarely the problem — the query, the index, the key, or the assumption was.

One habit prevents most of it: before any storage change, write down the specific query, its current plan, its volume, and the number it needs to hit. If you can't fill in that sentence, the change is a guess.

14 Interview Essentials & Level Expectations

Database choice comes up in essentially every system design interview, usually as "what would you store this in?" The trap is answering with a product name instead of a reason.

Mid-Level (E4)

Know the Toolkit

Explain ACID and what an index does. Distinguish the four NoSQL families and give a use case for each. Know that read replicas scale reads and sharding scales writes. Recognise the N+1 query and how to fix it.

80% Concepts · 20% Trade-offs
Senior (E5)

Justify the Order

Start from access patterns, not products. Walk the ladder — tune, cache, replicate, federate, denormalize, shard — and say why each step comes where it does. Defend a shard key. Name what federation costs you in joins and transactions.

50% Design · 50% Depth
Staff+ (E6)

Own the Data Architecture

Design a polyglot topology and defend every store in it. Plan a live migration with dual writes, verification, and a rollback path. Reason about hot tenants, resharding, and cross-store consistency. Know when the senior answer is "one Postgres, tuned properly."

30% Design · 70% Depth

Questions Worth Rehearsing

  • "SQL or NoSQL for this?" — Depends on the access patterns. Name the top queries first, then answer per dataset. Volume alone doesn't decide it.
  • "The database is slow. What do you do?" — EXPLAIN the slowest queries by total time consumed. Index, then cache, then replicate. Architecture last.
  • "How would you shard this?" — Pick a high-cardinality, evenly-distributed key that appears in most queries. Then immediately say what breaks: cross-shard joins, transactions, global uniqueness, and what happens when one tenant gets huge.
  • "Master-slave or master-master?" — Master-slave unless writes are geographically partitioned. Master-master's write conflicts are a real cost and it doesn't double write throughput.
  • "When would you denormalize?" — When a measured hot query is join-bound and already indexed, and reads outnumber writes heavily. Prefer a materialized view so the database maintains the copy.
  • "Users say their update disappeared." — Replica lag plus a read routed to a lagging replica. Fix with read-your-writes routing, not by abandoning replicas.
💡 Senior Signal

Volunteer what you're giving up. "I'd shard orders by customer_id — it's high cardinality and it's in most of our queries, so reads hit one shard. What I lose is cross-customer reporting, which becomes a scatter-gather, so I'd feed those into a separate analytics store. And I'd cap any single customer's share before it becomes a hot shard."

Anyone can pick a shard key. The second half of that answer is what distinguishes someone who has operated one.

15 Summary

The database conversation goes wrong when it starts with products. It goes right when it starts with the five questions from §01 — access patterns, read/write ratio, what must be transactional, volume and growth, and the shape of the data.

The compressed version: relational databases give you transactions, joins, constraints, and a query planner, and they scale much further than the reflex assumes — through tuning, caching, replication, federation, denormalization, and finally sharding, in that order of cost. NoSQL isn't one thing; it's four families that each drop different guarantees to buy different capabilities. Choose per dataset, and make every extra store justify itself against a specific query you can name.

💡 Final Thought

The most useful thing you can bring to a database decision isn't knowledge of exotic stores. It's the discipline to ask what the top five queries are, run EXPLAIN on the slow one, and say out loud what the proposed change costs — in joins, in transactions, in operational surface.

Most of the time that conversation ends with "index it and revisit in a year," and that's a good outcome. The systems that age well are usually the ones that stayed boring for as long as they honestly could.
