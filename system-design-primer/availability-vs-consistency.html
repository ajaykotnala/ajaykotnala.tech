Availability vs Consistency — The Choice You Make at 3 A.M.

A cable gets cut. Half your cluster can't see the other half. Right now, a customer is clicking "Buy" — and your system has exactly two options: answer with data it can't verify, or refuse to answer at all. That's the whole trade. Here's CAP stated properly, what CP and AP really look like in production, and what the nines actually cost.

01 The Choice Only a Failure Forces

On a normal day, this trade-off doesn't exist. Your database is consistent and available at the same time, all your replicas agree, every read returns the latest write, and nobody has to choose anything. That's the whole reason this topic is so slippery — for 99.9% of the year, the tension is invisible.

Then a switch fails. Or a router pushes a bad config. Or a cloud provider's internal network hiccups for ninety seconds. Suddenly one half of your cluster can't reach the other half, and neither half can tell whether the other one is dead or just unreachable — because from the inside, those two situations produce exactly the same silence.

In that moment, a request arrives at a node. And the node has two options, both bad:

  • Answer anyway, using whatever data it has locally — which may be stale, and which may conflict with a write that just landed on the other side of the split. The system stays up. The answer might be wrong.
  • Refuse to answer until it can confirm with its peers what the truth is. The answer is never wrong. But right now, to the user, the system is down.

That's it. That's the entire availability-versus-consistency trade, and there is no third option. Every distributed database you've ever used has picked a side, and most of them let you pick a different side per query if you know where the knob is.

What follows is the long version: what these words mean precisely (they are more specific than the everyday English suggests), what CAP actually claims versus what people think it claims, the full ladder of consistency models between "no guarantee" and "linearizable," and the availability patterns — fail-over and replication — that determine which side you land on whether you chose it or not.

💡 The One-Line Version

Consistency and availability only conflict during a partition. The rest of the time you're trading consistency against latency instead — which is the more common trade, and the one CAP never mentions.

02 What "Available" and "Consistent" Actually Mean

Both words are used loosely in ordinary conversation and precisely in the theory, and the mismatch causes most of the confusion in this area. Worth pinning down before going further.

Consistency (the CAP kind)

In CAP, consistency means linearizability: the system behaves as though there is exactly one copy of the data, and every operation takes effect at some single instant between when it was invoked and when it returned. If a write completes at 10:00:00.000, every read that starts after that instant must see it — regardless of which node serves the read.

That's a much stronger claim than "the data isn't corrupt." It's a claim about time and ordering across machines, and it's expensive precisely because machines don't share a clock.

⚠️ Three Different Things Called "Consistency"

CAP's C is linearizability — a distributed ordering guarantee. ACID's C is something completely different: it means a transaction moves the database from one valid state to another, respecting your declared invariants (foreign keys, check constraints). Cache consistency is a third thing again — whether a copy matches its source.

They share a word and nothing else. When someone says "we need a consistent database," it's always worth asking which of the three they mean, because the answers cost wildly different amounts.

Availability (the CAP kind)

In CAP, availability means every request to a non-failing node gets a non-error response — in finite time. Note what it doesn't say: it says nothing about latency, and nothing about the answer being correct or current. A node that returns thirty-second-old data in 2ms is available. A node that would return perfect data but blocks for 60 seconds waiting for a quorum is, formally, not.

This is narrower than the operational meaning of availability, which is usually "the percentage of time the service works" — the nines. Both meanings matter in this article; §11 handles the operational one in detail.

Partition Tolerance

A partition is when messages between nodes are lost or arbitrarily delayed. Partition tolerance means the system keeps operating despite that — it doesn't corrupt itself, doesn't hang forever, doesn't require a human to reconcile it by hand.

The critical point, which the popular framing gets wrong: partition tolerance is not something you choose. Networks fail. Cables get cut, switches reboot, garbage collection pauses look exactly like a dead node, cross-region links flap. If your data lives on more than one machine, you will experience partitions whether or not your design acknowledges them.

Property Formal Meaning What It Costs
Consistency Every read returns the most recent completed write — one logical copy of the data Coordination on every operation; unavailability during a partition
Availability Every request to a live node returns a non-error response in finite time Possibly stale or conflicting answers; reconciliation logic you must write
Partition tolerance The system keeps working when messages between nodes are lost or delayed Not a choice — it's a property of reality that you either handle or don't

03 The CAP Theorem, Stated Properly

You've seen the triangle. Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance — "pick two." It's on every interview prep site, and it's a genuinely misleading way to say a genuinely important thing.

The problem with "pick two" is that it implies three viable combinations: CA, CP, and AP. But CA — a distributed system that is consistent and available and simply doesn't tolerate partitions — isn't a design choice. It's a system that will violate one of its two promises the moment the network misbehaves, and won't have decided in advance which one.

Here's the more useful statement. It comes from the same theorem, just phrased as an operating rule rather than a menu:

CAP, Usefully Stated 
IF a network partition occurs, THEN a distributed system must
choose between:

    consistency  — refuse requests it cannot verify (stay correct, go down)
    availability — serve requests from local state  (stay up, risk wrong)

There is no configuration in which it can do both.
When there is NO partition, it can do both — and usually does.
Diagram 1 — The Partition Moment: Two Nodes, One Impossible Question

The node has local state and no way to verify it. Answering and refusing are both defensible — but they are different products, and the choice belongs to the business, not to the database's default config.

Diagram 2 — The Triangle, Corrected: P Is a Condition, Not a Choice

CAP is not a menu you order from once. It's a rule about what your system is permitted to do during a specific, temporary, guaranteed-to-happen failure.

💡 How to Say This in an Interview

"CAP says that when the network partitions, I have to choose between consistency and availability — I can't have both. Partition tolerance isn't optional in a distributed system, so the real question is always CP or AP, and I'd answer it differently for the payments path than for the activity feed."

That framing signals more than reciting the triangle ever will, because it shows you know the theorem describes failure-time behaviour rather than a permanent architectural identity.

Where CAP came from — and what its author said twelve years later

+

Eric Brewer proposed CAP as a conjecture in 2000, framing it as a rule of thumb for building large distributed services. Gilbert and Lynch formalised and proved it in 2002, which is where the precise definitions — linearizability for C, every-request-gets-a-response for A — come from.

In 2012 Brewer published a follow-up making a point that the popular version had lost: the "two out of three" formulation is misleading, because the choice is only forced during a partition, partitions are rare, and systems can and should behave differently at different times and for different operations. He also emphasised the part almost nobody implements — that a well-designed system should detect the partition, enter an explicit degraded mode, and then run a deliberate recovery process when the partition heals.

That third phase is where most real designs are weakest. Teams choose AP, the partition heals, and then discover that "reconcile the divergent writes" was never actually built — it was assumed to be the database's problem.

04 CP — Refuse Rather Than Guess

A CP system's promise is simple: I will never tell you something that isn't true. When it can't guarantee that, it stops answering.

Mechanically, this almost always means a majority quorum. The cluster elects a leader, writes go through it, and a write is only acknowledged once a majority of nodes have durably recorded it. When the network splits, arithmetic does the deciding: in a five-node cluster split three-two, the three-node side still has a majority and keeps operating. The two-node side knows it's in the minority, and voluntarily steps down — refusing reads and writes rather than risk serving a stale answer or accepting a write that will later conflict.

That voluntary step-down is the whole trick. A minority partition could keep serving. It chooses not to, because a system that lets both sides act is a system that will need a human to untangle it later.

Diagram 3 — CP Under Partition: Majority Rules, Minority Stands Down

CP doesn't mean the whole system goes down during a partition. It means the side that can't prove it's current takes itself out of service, so the side that can keeps a single version of the truth.

What CP Feels Like in Production

  • Writes need a round trip to a quorum, so write latency includes the slowest node in the majority. Across regions, that's tens of milliseconds minimum.
  • Leader elections cause brief write outages. When a leader dies, there's a detection timeout plus an election — typically a few seconds during which writes fail.
  • Adding nodes doesn't add write throughput. Every write still goes through one leader and still needs a majority. More nodes means a bigger majority to convince.
  • Errors are honest. When something is wrong, you get an error rather than a wrong answer, which makes debugging vastly easier.

The systems in this family are the ones you reach for when the data is the point: etcd and ZooKeeper (cluster coordination, service discovery, distributed locks), Consul, HBase, Google Spanner and its descendants, CockroachDB, MongoDB with majority write concern and majority read concern, and any classic relational database with synchronous replication.

💡 Where CP Is Non-Negotiable

Anything where two conflicting answers cause real-world damage: account balances and payments, inventory for scarce items, seat and room booking, uniqueness constraints (usernames, order IDs), leader election and distributed locks, and permission checks. If the failure mode is "we sold the same thing twice" or "we let someone in who shouldn't be," you want the system to fail closed.

05 AP — Answer Now, Reconcile Later

An AP system makes the opposite promise: I will always answer. Every node keeps serving from whatever it knows, on both sides of a partition, and when the network heals the system works out what happened.

There's no leader to lose and no quorum to fail, so there's nothing to elect and nothing to block on. Any node can accept a write. Which means during a partition, both sides accept writes — and the data genuinely diverges. That divergence isn't a bug; it's the design. The interesting engineering is entirely in what happens next.

Diagram 4 — AP Under Partition: Diverge, Then Reconcile

The shopping cart is the canonical AP example precisely because union-merging is a defensible business answer. A bank balance has no equivalent — which is why the same technique doesn't transfer.

The Part People Skip: Conflict Resolution

Choosing AP means signing up to answer "what happens when two versions disagree?" There are four common answers and they are not equivalent.

⏱️

Last-Write-Wins

Highest timestamp survives. Trivial to implement, and silently discards the other write.

🔀

Version Vectors

Detects genuine concurrency instead of guessing. Hands the conflict to your code.

🧮

CRDTs

Data types whose merge is mathematically defined. Converge without asking anyone.

👤

Keep Siblings

Store both, surface both. The user picks — like a Git merge conflict.

Last-write-wins deserves a specific warning, because it's the default in more systems than you'd like. It depends on comparing wall-clock timestamps from different machines, and machine clocks drift. If node B's clock is 200ms ahead of node A's, a write that genuinely happened earlier on B can beat a later write on A. The result is silent data loss with no error, no log line, and no way to reconstruct what was dropped.

CRDTs — conflict-free replicated data types — are the elegant answer when your data fits one. A grow-only counter, an add-wins set, a last-writer-wins register with proper causality tracking: these have merge functions that are commutative, associative, and idempotent, which means replicas converge to the same value no matter what order updates arrive in. Collaborative editors and offline-first apps lean on them heavily.

The AP family includes Cassandra, DynamoDB (tunable, but AP by default), Riak, CouchDB, Redis in cluster mode with async replication, and — the biggest one of all — DNS, which is eventually consistent by design and has never had a global outage because of it.

🔶 Where AP Is Clearly Right

Anything where being slightly stale is invisible or harmless, and being down is expensive: social feeds, like and view counts, product catalogues, recommendations, search indexes, session data, analytics ingestion, notification delivery, and content distribution. Nobody has ever filed a support ticket because a view count was three seconds behind. They file tickets when the page doesn't load.

06 PACELC — The Half Everyone Forgets

Here's the honest problem with CAP as a design tool: it only describes what happens during a partition, and partitions are rare. It says nothing about the other 99.9% of the time — which is when you're actually running your business.

Daniel Abadi's PACELC extension fills that gap, and it's the version I'd rather people memorised:

PACELC 
if (P)  →  choose  A  or  C      // during a Partition: Availability or Consistency
ELSE    →  choose  L  or  C      // otherwise: Latency or Consistency

The second clause is the one that shapes your system every single day. Even with a perfectly healthy network, strong consistency requires coordination — a write must reach a quorum, a read must confirm it isn't stale — and coordination costs round trips. If your replicas span three regions, every strongly consistent write pays inter-region latency, whether or not anything is broken.

So the everyday trade isn't availability versus consistency at all. It's latency versus consistency — which connects directly to the latency and throughput material: coordination is serial work on the critical path, and serial work is latency.

Diagram 5 — PACELC: Two Questions, Not One

The ELSE branch is where your system lives day to day. A database that is "CP" can still hand you stale reads from a replica — that's the EL half of its personality, and it's usually configurable.

💡 The Question Behind the Question

When someone asks "is your system CP or AP?", the more useful answer describes both halves: "During a partition we favour consistency on the ledger and availability on the catalogue. On the happy path we read from local replicas everywhere except checkout, which reads from the leader and pays about 40ms for it."

07 Consistency Patterns: Weak, Eventual, Strong

"Consistent" and "not consistent" is a false binary. There's a full ladder between them, and the rungs have names because each one is a specific, useful promise that costs a specific amount.

Weak Consistency — No Promise At All

After a write, reads may or may not see it. There's no guarantee of convergence, no guarantee of ordering, and no bound on how long staleness lasts. If the data is lost or never propagates, that's within spec.

This sounds useless until you notice where it's exactly right: real-time media. In a video call or a multiplayer game, a packet from 400ms ago has no value — you don't want it retransmitted, you want the current frame. Weak consistency is the correct model whenever fresh beats complete. Memcached without invalidation, UDP-based telemetry, and live position updates in games all live here.

Eventual Consistency — Convergence, Eventually

Stop writing, wait long enough, and all replicas will agree. That's the entire promise: convergence in the absence of new writes, with no bound on how long "eventually" takes and no guarantee about what you see along the way.

In practice, "eventually" is usually milliseconds — most eventually consistent systems converge faster than a human can perceive. The problem is that "usually" isn't a guarantee, and the tail is where surprises live: a replica lagging thirty seconds behind during a rebuild, a cross-region link saturated by a backfill, a node that rejoined and is still catching up.

DNS is the classic example — you update a record, and resolvers worldwide pick it up over the TTL window. Email delivery, CDN invalidation, and most analytics pipelines work the same way.

⚠️ "Eventual" Has No Deadline

Plain eventual consistency gives you no upper bound. If you need one — and you usually do — measure your replication lag as a distribution and set an SLO on it, exactly as you would for latency. "p99 replication lag under 500ms, alert at 2 seconds" is an engineering statement. "Eventually consistent" is not.

The Session Guarantees In Between

Between raw eventual consistency and full linearizability sits a set of much cheaper guarantees that fix the specific anomalies users actually notice. These are the ones worth knowing by name, because they're usually the right answer.

  • Read-your-writes — you always see your own updates, even if others don't yet. Fixes the single most common complaint in a replicated system: "I saved it and it disappeared."
  • Monotonic reads — you never see time go backwards. Once you've read a value, no subsequent read shows you an older one. Fixes the flicker where refreshing a page toggles between two versions because two replicas served the two requests.
  • Monotonic writes — your own writes apply in the order you issued them.
  • Causal consistency — operations that are causally related appear in that order to everyone. If a comment references a post, nobody sees the comment before the post. Unrelated operations can be seen in any order, which is what makes it far cheaper than linearizability.

Causal consistency is a genuinely underused sweet spot. It rules out the anomalies that look like bugs to a user, while still allowing every node to answer locally in the common case.

Strong Consistency — Behaves Like One Machine

After a write completes, every subsequent read sees it, from any node, no exceptions. The system is indistinguishable from a single machine that processes operations one at a time.

The cost is coordination on the critical path: consensus for writes, and reads that either go through the leader or confirm leadership before answering. That's round trips, and during a partition it means the minority side stops.

Diagram 6 — The Consistency Ladder: What Each Rung Buys and Costs

The ladder is the real decision space. "Do we need strong consistency?" is usually answered best with "no — we need read-your-writes on this one endpoint," which is a far cheaper commitment.

Java — Read-Your-Writes Without Going Fully Strong 
// The cheapest fix for "I saved it and it vanished": after a write,
// pin THIS user's reads to a replica that has caught up to the
// version they just created. Everyone else keeps reading locally.

public Order placeOrder(Order order, Session session) {
    long version = primary.write(order);      // returns a monotonic token
    session.setMinVersion("orders", version);   // remember what they've seen
    return order;
}

public List<Order> listOrders(Session session) {
    long required = session.getMinVersion("orders");

    Replica replica = replicaPool.pickCaughtUpTo(required);
    if (replica == null) {
        // No replica is current enough — fall back to the leader.
        // Slower, but never shows this user a past they've moved beyond.
        return primary.list();
    }
    return replica.list();
}

08 Tunable Consistency and Quorum Math

The best part of this whole topic is that in many systems, the CP-versus-AP choice isn't baked into the database at all — it's three numbers you set per query.

The Quorum Rule 
N  =  number of replicas holding each piece of data
W  =  replicas that must acknowledge a WRITE before it returns
R  =  replicas that must respond to a READ before it returns

IF  R + W > N   the read set and the write set must overlap,
                so every read touches at least one replica
                carrying the latest acknowledged write.

The overlap is the entire idea. If a write landed on at least W replicas and a read consults R replicas, and those two numbers sum to more than N, then by the pigeonhole principle at least one replica in the read set has the new value. Attach a version to each value and the reader can tell which one is newest.

Diagram 7 — Quorum Overlap: Three Ways to Configure the Same Cluster

The same three machines can behave as a CP store or an AP store depending on two integers in your client config. That's why "is Cassandra AP?" is only half a question.

⚠️ R + W > N Is Weaker Than It Looks

Quorum overlap guarantees a read sees the newest acknowledged write. It does not give you linearizability on its own. Concurrent writes to the same key can still both succeed on overlapping-but-different quorums, leaving two versions that only a version vector or a merge function can settle.

It also breaks under "sloppy quorums" — where a system accepts writes on substitute nodes when the real replicas are unreachable, then hands the data back later. That's a deliberate availability choice, and it means W acknowledgements may not be W of the right nodes.

Per-Query Consistency in Practice 
-- Cassandra: the level is set per statement, not per cluster

-- Payment ledger — must not lose or duplicate
CONSISTENCY QUORUM;
INSERT INTO ledger (txn_id, account, amount) VALUES (...);

-- Page view counter — being 2 seconds stale is invisible
CONSISTENCY ONE;
UPDATE counters SET views = views + 1 WHERE page_id = ...;

-- Cross-region config read that must reflect a global change
CONSISTENCY EACH_QUORUM;
SELECT * FROM feature_flags WHERE tenant = ...;

How AP systems catch up: read repair, hinted handoff, anti-entropy

+

Read repair — when a read consults several replicas and finds them disagreeing, the coordinator returns the newest value and quietly writes it back to the stale replicas. Convergence rides along on normal traffic, which means frequently-read data heals fast and cold data doesn't heal at all.

Hinted handoff — if a replica is down when a write arrives, a peer stores a "hint" (this write belongs to node 3) and replays it when node 3 returns. This is what keeps writes available during short outages, and it's also what makes the quorum guarantee sloppy: the write was acknowledged, but not by the node that's supposed to own it.

Anti-entropy repair — a background process that compares replicas wholesale using Merkle trees, so differences can be found without shipping all the data, and repairs everything including the keys nobody reads. It's expensive, it's usually scheduled weekly, and skipping it is how teams discover deleted rows resurrecting themselves months later.

09 Availability Pattern 1 — Fail-Over

Availability in practice comes from two patterns: fail-over (something breaks, something else takes over) and replication (multiple copies exist so no single loss is fatal). They're related but distinct, and each has its own consistency cost.

Active-Passive

One server handles all traffic. A second sits idle, receiving replicated data and exchanging heartbeats. When heartbeats stop, the passive server promotes itself, takes over the virtual IP or updates the service registry, and starts serving.

The standby comes in three temperatures, and the difference is entirely recovery time: hot (fully running, data current, takeover in seconds), warm (running but data slightly behind, takeover in minutes), cold (provisioned on demand from backups, takeover in hours). Each step down is cheaper to run and slower to recover — which is exactly the RTO/RPO conversation, wearing different clothes.

Active-Active

Both servers take live traffic. There's no promotion step, so failure means the load balancer simply stops routing to the dead node — recovery is measured in the health-check interval rather than in a failover procedure. You also get the capacity of both machines instead of paying for one to idle.

The catch: each node must be able to carry the full load alone, or losing one just moves the outage from "unavailable" to "overloaded" — the utilization cliff from the latency primer, arriving on schedule. And if both nodes accept writes, you're now running a multi-primary system with all the conflict handling that implies.

Diagram 8 — Fail-Over Modes, and the Failure Mode of Fail-Over Itself

Fail-over machinery is itself a source of outages: it runs rarely, is hard to test, and its worst failure mode — two nodes both believing they're primary — is worse than the failure it was built to handle.

⚠️ You Cannot Distinguish "Dead" From "Slow"

This is the deepest reason distributed systems are hard. A node that has crashed and a node that is paused, overloaded, or behind a broken link look identical from the outside: silence. Every failure detector is therefore a guess with a timeout attached — set it short and you get false promotions, set it long and you get long outages.

Because the guess can be wrong, correctness must not depend on it. That's what fencing tokens are for: even if a stale primary wakes up and tries to write, the storage layer rejects it because its generation number is old. The detector is allowed to be wrong; the data isn't.

10 Availability Pattern 2 — Replication

Replication is where availability and consistency meet most directly, because the single question "when do we acknowledge a write?" determines both how fast you are and how much you can lose.

Primary-Replica

One node accepts writes and streams its changes to read-only replicas. Reads scale horizontally — add replicas, serve more reads. Writes don't scale at all; there's exactly one writer.

The consistency behaviour depends entirely on the acknowledgement mode:

Mode Primary acks when… Write latency Data loss on primary failure
Asynchronous Its own disk write completes Fastest — local only Anything not yet shipped is gone
Semi-synchronous At least one replica confirms receipt One extra round trip Bounded — one replica always has it
Synchronous All (or a quorum of) replicas confirm Slowest node in the set None

Async replication is the default in most setups, and it's the reason "we failed over successfully" and "we lost 4 seconds of orders" are compatible statements. Semi-sync is the pragmatic middle: one extra hop in exchange for a hard bound on what a failover can lose.

Multi-Primary

Every node accepts writes and replicates to the others. Writes scale, no promotion is needed on failure, and users can write to whichever region they're nearest. It's also the configuration where conflicts stop being theoretical: two regions can update the same row within the replication window and both believe they succeeded.

Multi-primary is genuinely the right choice for partitioned workloads — where each region naturally owns its own rows and cross-region writes to the same key are rare. It's a trap when applied to a globally contended dataset, because then you're paying for conflict resolution on every hot key.

Diagram 9 — Replication Topologies and Where the Lag Hides

Replication lag is not a single number — it's per-replica, and the worst replica defines the worst user experience. Monitor the maximum, not the average.

11 Availability in Numbers

Time to make availability concrete. "Highly available" means nothing; a percentage with a time window means something, and the jump between adjacent percentages is much larger than it looks.

Availability Name Downtime / year Downtime / month Downtime / day
99% Two nines 3d 15h 36m 7h 12m 14m 24s
99.9% Three nines 8h 46m 43m 12s 1m 26s
99.95% Three and a half 4h 23m 21m 36s 43s
99.99% Four nines 52m 34s 4m 19s 8.6s
99.999% Five nines 5m 15s 26s 0.9s

Read that table with an on-call rota in mind. At three nines, you can absorb a 40-minute incident once a month and still hit target. At four nines, your entire annual budget is 52 minutes — which means a single bad deploy that takes an hour to roll back has blown the year. At five nines, no human can be in the recovery loop at all; if a person has to read a page, decide, and act, you've already spent the budget.

How Availability Composes

Individual component numbers are only useful if you know how they combine, and there are exactly two rules.

Composition 
IN SEQUENCE  (a dependency chain — all must work)
    A_total = A₁ × A₂ × A₃ × …
    99.99% × 99.99% = 99.98%          // worse than either part
    five services at 99.99% = 99.95%   // 4h 23m/year

IN PARALLEL  (redundant copies — any one suffices)
    A_total = 1 − (1 − A₁) × (1 − A₂) × …
    99.9% ∥ 99.9%   = 99.9999%        // better than either part
    99.9% ∥ 99.9% ∥ 99.9% = 99.9999999%
Diagram 10 — The Nines, and How They Add Up (or Don't)

Serial composition is the number most teams forget: a service that depends on six others at 99.99% each cannot itself promise 99.99%, no matter how good its own code is.

⚠️ The Parallel Formula Lies to You

Redundancy multiplies availability only when failures are independent. Two replicas in the same rack share a power feed and a top-of-rack switch. Two instances in the same availability zone share a control plane. Two services running the same code share the same bug — deploy it to both and your "redundant" pair fails simultaneously.

Correlated failure is the reason real systems never reach the numbers the arithmetic promises. When you claim redundancy, name what the copies don't share — that's the only part that actually counts.

Turning the nines into a decision: error budgets

+

An availability target is also a permission slip. If your SLO is 99.9%, you are explicitly allowed 43 minutes of downtime per month — that's the error budget, and spending it is not a failure, it's the plan.

The useful part is what it does to arguments. When the budget is healthy, ship faster and take more risk: the budget exists to be used. When it's exhausted, feature work pauses and reliability work takes priority — automatically, without anyone having to win a debate about whether stability matters this quarter.

It also forces honesty about targets. Teams asking for four nines usually revise the ask once they see it means 52 minutes a year across every deploy, dependency, migration, and cloud incident combined — and that each additional nine roughly multiplies the infrastructure and process cost.

12 Choose Per Operation, Not Per System

Here's the conclusion that most of this article has been building toward, and the thing that separates a textbook answer from a design that survives contact with a real product.

"Is our system CP or AP?" is the wrong question. Almost every real product needs both, because different operations have genuinely different consequences when they're wrong. The right question is asked one endpoint at a time: what happens if this specific operation returns stale data, and what happens if it returns an error? Whichever answer is worse, you avoid.

Diagram 11 — One Product, Both Answers: A Per-Operation Map

Both columns belong to one product and frequently to one database. The classification is per operation, driven by the consequence of being wrong — not by a company-wide architectural stance.

The Pattern That Gets You Both

The best designs don't just split operations — they deliberately move work off the consistent path so the consistent path stays small.

  • Reserve, don't decrement. Take a short-lived hold on inventory (strongly consistent, tiny, fast), then let the rest of checkout run on eventually consistent data. The expensive guarantee applies to one operation, not to the whole flow.
  • Accept first, validate after. An order can be accepted optimistically and cancelled seconds later if validation fails — a rare, cheap apology in exchange for an always- available submit button. This is exactly how airline overbooking works, and it is a deliberate business decision rather than a bug.
  • Make writes idempotent. With an idempotency key, a retry can't double-apply. That removes the main danger of the AP path — and it's the single highest-value habit on this entire list.
  • Degrade rather than fail. If the recommendation service is partitioned, render the page without recommendations. Availability is not binary; a partially working page is worth far more than an error page.
  • Shrink the consistent core. Keep the linearizable footprint as small as possible — often just a ledger, a lock table, and a uniqueness index. Everything else can be derived from it asynchronously.
Java — Idempotency Key: Making Retries Safe on the AP Path 
// The client generates a key once and reuses it on every retry.
// A duplicate request returns the ORIGINAL result — it never
// performs the side effect twice, no matter how the network behaved.

public Payment charge(ChargeRequest req) {
    String key = req.idempotencyKey();      // e.g. a client-side UUID

    // Atomic claim on the strongly consistent store. Only one caller wins.
    boolean claimed = ledger.putIfAbsent(key, Status.IN_PROGRESS);
    if (!claimed) {
        Payment existing = ledger.awaitResult(key, Duration.ofSeconds(5));
        return existing;                  // the retry gets the first answer
    }

    Payment result = gateway.execute(req);
    ledger.complete(key, result);
    return result;
}

// Without this, "the request timed out — retry it" is indistinguishable
// from "the request succeeded and the ACK was lost", and the customer
// gets charged twice. Idempotency is what makes retries a safe default.

13 Five Ways This Goes Wrong in Production

The same material, expressed as incidents. Each of these is common enough that you'll recognise at least one.

1. "I Saved It and It Disappeared"

A user updates their profile, the write goes to the primary, and the confirmation page reads from a replica that hasn't caught up. They see their old data and conclude the save failed — so they do it again, sometimes with different values, and now you have a real ordering problem on top of a perceived one. This is the most common consistency bug in existence, and it's fixed by read-your-writes: after a write, route that user's reads to the primary or to a replica known to be caught up, for a short window.

2. Split-Brain After a Failover

The primary experiences a 25-second GC pause. The failure detector concludes it's dead and promotes the standby. The old primary wakes up with no idea anything happened and keeps serving writes to the clients still connected to it. Two primaries, two divergent write streams, and a manual reconciliation in your future. Fixed by quorum-gated promotion plus fencing tokens — the storage layer must reject writes carrying a stale generation number, no matter how confident the sender is.

3. Last-Write-Wins Ate the Data

Two writes to the same key, milliseconds apart, on different nodes. The system compares wall-clock timestamps to decide the winner. But node B's clock drifted 300ms ahead, so the write that genuinely happened second loses, silently. No error, no conflict, no log line — just an update that quietly didn't happen. Fixed by using logical clocks or version vectors to detect true concurrency, and by choosing a merge strategy on purpose instead of inheriting the default.

4. The Retry That Charged Twice

A payment request succeeds, but the response is lost in a network blip. The client times out and retries. The second request also succeeds. The customer is charged twice, and the fact that both requests were "correct" is no comfort at all. Fixed by idempotency keys — and note that this is fundamentally a consistency problem created by an availability mechanism, which is why the two topics can't be reasoned about separately.

5. The Redundancy That Wasn't

Three replicas, all in the same availability zone, sharing a control plane. The zone has an incident and all three go down simultaneously. The availability arithmetic promised six nines; the correlated failure delivered one shared outage. Fixed by spreading replicas across genuinely independent failure domains — different zones, different power, different network paths — and by being explicit about what the copies still share, because something always does.

⚠️ The Common Thread

Four of these five are not database bugs. They're gaps between what the database guarantees and what the application assumed it guaranteed. The database did exactly what it promised — the promise was just weaker than someone believed.

Which makes the highest-value habit in this whole topic a documentation habit: for each critical endpoint, write down the consistency level it actually gets, and what a user sees when it's violated. Most teams have never written that down, and most incidents in this space start there.

14 Interview Essentials & Level Expectations

CAP shows up in nearly every system design interview, usually as a trap: the interviewer wants to see whether you recite the triangle or reason about the trade.

Mid-Level (E4)

Know the Trade

State CAP correctly, including that P isn't optional. Distinguish eventual from strong consistency and give an example of each. Explain why a read replica can serve stale data. Know that async replication means a failover can lose writes.

80% Concepts · 20% Trade-offs
Senior (E5)

Own the Decision

Classify operations individually rather than labelling the whole system. Use quorum math to justify a configuration. Name read-your-writes as the cheap fix for the common complaint. Bring up PACELC without prompting, and say what your happy-path read costs.

50% Design · 50% Depth
Staff+ (E6)

Design the Recovery

Specify the conflict-resolution strategy before the partition, not after. Design the fencing that makes a wrong failure detection survivable. Reason about correlated failure when claiming redundancy. Set the org's SLO and error-budget policy, and shrink the strongly consistent core to the smallest defensible surface.

30% Design · 70% Depth

Questions Worth Rehearsing

  • "Is your design CP or AP?" — Per operation. Payments and inventory are CP; catalogue, feed, and search are AP. Then say what each one does when the partition hits.
  • "Why can't you have all three?" — During a partition, a node either answers from unverified local state (giving up C) or refuses (giving up A). There's no third behaviour. Outside a partition you have both.
  • "How do you stop split-brain?" — Quorum-gated promotion so a minority can never elect itself, plus fencing tokens so a resurrected old primary gets rejected by storage.
  • "Users say their update vanished. What's happening?" — Replica lag plus a read routed to a lagging replica. Fix with read-your-writes, not with full strong consistency.
  • "What availability can you promise?" — No more than the product of everything you depend on. Name the chain first, then the number.
  • "Eventually consistent — how eventual?" — Measure replication lag as a distribution and put an SLO on its p99. If nobody can answer this, that's the finding.
💡 Senior Signal

Volunteer the recovery plan. Anyone can say "we'll go AP for the cart." The senior version continues: "…which means during a partition both regions accept cart writes, and on heal we union-merge because carts are add-only. Removals get a tombstone with a version vector so a delete doesn't get resurrected by a stale add."

That second half is the part most candidates never reach, and it's the part that decides whether the design actually works.

15 Summary

Availability and consistency aren't opposing philosophies. They're two things you get for free most of the time, and must choose between during a specific, temporary, guaranteed-to-happen failure — a network partition.

The compressed version: CAP forces the choice only when the network breaks, and partition tolerance isn't optional, so the real question is always CP or AP. PACELC covers the other 99.9% of the time, where you're trading consistency against latency instead. There's a full ladder between weak and strong, and the cheap middle rungs — read-your-writes, monotonic reads, causal — fix the anomalies users actually report. Availability comes from fail-over and replication, both of which have their own failure modes, and the nines multiply along a dependency chain in a way that caps your ambition whether you planned for it or not.

💡 Final Thought

The engineers who handle this well aren't the ones who memorised the triangle. They're the ones who, before the partition ever happens, have already written down what each endpoint does when it can't reach its peers — and what the system does when they can talk again. The first half is design. The second half is the one everybody skips, and it's the one that decides how the incident ends.
