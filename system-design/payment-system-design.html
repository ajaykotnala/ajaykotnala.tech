Designing a Payment System for India:
A System Design Deep Dive

It's 11:59 PM, one minute before the festive sale opens. Lakhs of shoppers are about to tap Pay at the same moment — most of them on UPI, many on patchy 4G. Some payments will succeed but look failed, some will hang in "processing", and a few will get tapped twice. Let's build the payment system that moves every rupee exactly once and can prove it the next morning — from idempotency keys and a double-entry ledger to UPI pending states, reconciliation, and seller payouts.

01Understanding the Problem

Picture ApnaBazaar, a fictional online marketplace. It has 3 lakh sellers — block-printers in Jaipur, electronics wholesalers in Surat, spice traders in Kochi — and buyers everywhere from Bengaluru high-rises to tier-3 towns in Bihar. A buyer fills a cart with items from several sellers, taps Pay once, and ApnaBazaar pays each seller their share a week later, minus commission and taxes.

We're designing the payment backend behind that button. It takes money in from buyers (pay-in), keeps track of who it belongs to, and sends it out to sellers (pay-out). And it does this in India, which shapes almost every decision: UPI carries the large majority of payments, a "pending" UPI payment can take minutes to resolve, card numbers legally can't be stored by merchants, and payment data has to stay inside the country.

One thing to get straight early: ApnaBazaar doesn't move money itself. Only RBI-regulated entities touch the actual rails. Our system orchestrates them, records every movement, and proves the records are right.

Who What They Do
Buyer's bank Holds the buyer's money and approves or declines the debit
UPI app The app where the buyer enters their UPI PIN
NPCI Runs the UPI switch (and RuPay) that routes money between banks
Card networks Route card payments between the buyer's bank and the acquiring bank
Payment Aggregator (PA) An RBI-authorised company that connects us to all of the above through one API
Escrow bank Holds buyers' money in a dedicated account until sellers are paid
Seller's bank Receives the payout over IMPS, NEFT or RTGS
💡 Interview Tip

Before drawing anything, ask two questions: "Do we ever see card numbers?" and "Do we also pay the sellers?" The first decides your compliance burden. The second turns "call a payment gateway" into a bookkeeping problem — and that's where the real design is.

02Defining Requirements

Functional Requirements

Here's what the payment system has to do:

  • Pay-in — the buyer pays once for the whole cart, with UPI (intent or QR), cards, netbanking or wallets
  • Split by seller — one payment covers many seller orders, each with its own commission
  • Payment status — the buyer always sees success, failure, or an honest "processing"
  • Refunds — cancellations and returns go back to the original payment method
  • Payouts — sellers are paid into their bank accounts once the return window closes
  • Reconciliation — every day, our books are matched against the PA and the bank

Non-Functional Requirements

And these are the constraints that make it interesting:

Back-of-the-Envelope

Let's put numbers on an ordinary day and on the night the festive sale opens. Assume 20 lakh checkouts a day with an average cart of ₹1,200.

None of these numbers is scary. Even the sale-night peak is well within what a well-run relational database can handle. The hard part of a payment system is that its worst failures are silent: a debit with no order, an order with no debit, a seller paid twice. Nothing crashes, and the books are quietly wrong. So this design is about correctness first and throughput second.

03Core Entities & Data Model

Six nouns carry the whole design. Getting them straight early saves a lot of back-and-forth later.

🛒

Checkout

One cart, one total, and one payment the buyer approves. Identified by checkout_id.

📦

Seller Order

The slice of a checkout that belongs to one seller, with its own amount and commission.

🔁

Payment Attempt

One try at paying a checkout. A buyer whose first UPI try fails may make several.

🏦

Account

A named bucket of money: the escrow, one seller's payable balance, our commission.

📒

Ledger Entry

One line of a journal. Every journal's debits and credits add up to exactly zero.

💸

Payout

One bank transfer to a seller, usually covering many orders at once.

Two modelling decisions matter more than the rest.

One debit per checkout, split in the ledger. Meera's cart has a kurta from Jaipur and earphones from Surat. We don't ask her to approve two UPI payments — she enters her PIN once for ₹1,798. Splitting that money between the two sellers is bookkeeping we do internally, in the ledger.

Attempts are separate from checkouts. A noticeable share of first UPI attempts fail — wrong PIN, the bank's server is down, the buyer switches apps and never comes back. Each retry is a new attempt with its own reference at the PA. The checkout is paid when exactly one attempt succeeds.

For storage, we pick PostgreSQL. Payments need ACID transactions and strong consistency far more than raw speed. Postgres has a long track record in financial systems, mature tooling for backups and replication, and a large pool of engineers and DBAs who know it well. Those things matter more here than any benchmark.

Schema -- PostgreSQL, Mumbai region. All amounts are integer paise. CREATE TABLE checkouts ( checkout_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, buyer_id TEXT NOT NULL, amount_paise BIGINT NOT NULL, -- ₹1,798.00 = 179800 status TEXT NOT NULL, -- OPEN, PAID, EXPIRED expires_at TIMESTAMPTZ -- stock held until then ); CREATE TABLE seller_orders ( seller_order_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, checkout_id TEXT REFERENCES checkouts, seller_id TEXT NOT NULL, amount_paise BIGINT NOT NULL, commission_paise BIGINT NOT NULL ); CREATE TABLE payment_attempts ( attempt_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, -- our reference at the PA checkout_id TEXT REFERENCES checkouts, idempotency_key TEXT UNIQUE NOT NULL, method TEXT, -- UPI_INTENT, CARD, NETBANKING amount_paise BIGINT NOT NULL, status TEXT NOT NULL, -- CREATED, PENDING, SUCCESS, ... pa_payment_id TEXT UNIQUE, utr TEXT, -- bank reference, used in recon response JSONB, -- replayed to retries updated_at TIMESTAMPTZ ); CREATE TABLE ledger_entries ( -- append-only, never updated entry_id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY, journal_id TEXT NOT NULL, -- one business event account_id TEXT NOT NULL, -- 'seller_payable:S_JPR_221' direction TEXT NOT NULL, -- DEBIT or CREDIT amount_paise BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (amount_paise > 0), created_at TIMESTAMPTZ ); CREATE TABLE payment_outbox ( id BIGSERIAL, event_type TEXT, checkout_id TEXT, payload JSONB );
⚠️ Never Use Floats for Money

In floating point, 0.1 + 0.2 is 0.30000000000000004. Spread that over crores of transactions and the books stop balancing. Store integer paise in a BIGINT: ₹1,798.00 is 179800. In JSON, send it as an integer in paise or as a string — never as a float.

04The API Surface

Four endpoints cover the product: two for the buyer's app, one for refunds, and one internal endpoint where the PA tells us what happened.

POST /v1/checkouts/{checkoutId}/payments

Starts a payment attempt. The app generates an Idempotency-Key when the buyer taps Pay and sends the same key on every retry of that tap.

Request POST /v1/checkouts/chk_5TQ1/payments Idempotency-Key: 6f1c2a0e-3b7d-4d8e-9a51-2c4f7e1b9d30 { "method": "upi_intent", "amount_paise": 179800 }
Response · 201 { "attempt_id": "att_9KX2", "status": "PENDING", "next_action": { "type": "open_upi_app", "uri": "upi://pay?pa=apnabazaar@pabank&pn=ApnaBazaar&tr=att_9KX2&am=1798.00&cu=INR" }, "poll_after_ms": 2000 }
GET /v1/payments/{attemptId}

The current status of an attempt. The app polls this while the buyer is in their UPI app, and again when they come back.

POST /v1/payments/{attemptId}/refunds

Refunds all or part of a successful payment. It also takes an Idempotency-Key, because a refund sent twice is just as bad as a charge sent twice.

POST /internal/webhooks/pa

Where the PA reports results: payment captured, failed, refunded, settled. Every call carries an HMAC signature, which we verify before reading anything else in it.

Design Decision

Why does the request carry amount_paise if the server already knows the cart? It's only a cross-check. The server always recomputes the total from the cart. If the two differ — a price changed, or someone edited the request — it returns 409 Conflict and the app shows the new total. The amount we charge never comes from the client.

05High-Level Architecture

The system has two speeds. The payment path is what the buyer waits on: create an attempt, hand them to their UPI app, hear back from the PA. Everything else — the ledger, the order confirmation, the SMS — happens asynchronously after a payment is confirmed. And the actual money moves on a third clock entirely, a day or more later.

Diagram 1 — High-Level System Architecture

The indigo path is the only part the buyer waits on. Blue events fan out once a payment is confirmed. Gold is real money moving between bank accounts — on its own clock, days later.

Walking Through a Payment

  1. Meera in Pune taps Pay ₹1,798. Her app creates an idempotency key for this tap and calls POST /v1/checkouts/chk_5TQ1/payments.
  2. The payment service recomputes the cart total, asks the risk engine for a quick verdict (velocity, device, account age), and inserts an attempt row with status CREATED.
  3. The payment executor creates an order at the payment aggregator, passing att_9KX2 as its reference. The PA returns a UPI intent link, and the attempt moves to PENDING.
  4. Meera's phone opens her UPI app. She enters her PIN, and NPCI routes the debit from her bank to the PA's bank.
  5. The PA sends a signed webhook: captured, with the bank's UTR. The executor verifies the signature, marks the attempt SUCCESS, and writes an outbox row — in one transaction.
  6. Change data capture publishes the outbox row to Kafka. The ledger posts journal entries, the order service confirms both seller orders, and Meera gets an SMS.
  7. Her app, which was polling GET /v1/payments/att_9KX2, shows Order placed.
  8. The next day, the PA settles the money into our escrow account. A week later, once the return window closes, the payout service pays each seller their share.

Sync Where the Buyer Waits, Async Everywhere Else

It's tempting to make the payment service call the ledger, the order service and the SMS service one after another before replying. That works on day one and hurts on sale night.

Concern Synchronous Calls Events on Kafka
Latency Grows with every service in the chain The buyer waits only for the payment step
Failure isolation SMS provider down → checkout fails SMS provider down → SMS is late, payment is fine
Coupling Payment service must know every consumer New consumers just subscribe
Sale-night spikes No buffer, so overload cascades Kafka absorbs the burst, consumers catch up
Simplicity ● Easy to follow ○ Needs idempotent consumers and lag alerts

Notice how Kafka is used: as a broadcast, not a work queue. In a work queue, each message is handled by exactly one worker. Here, one "payment succeeded" event has to reach the ledger, the order service and the notifier, so each of them is its own consumer group and each sees every event.

⚠️ Watch Out

Async doesn't mean "fire and forget". If the payment service updated Postgres and then published to Kafka as a separate step, a crash in between would lose the event — a paid order that never gets confirmed. That's why the event goes into an outbox table in the same transaction as the status change, and change data capture (Debezium) publishes it from the database log.

06Deep Dive: Never Charge Twice

Here's a scenario that plays out every single day at our scale. Meera taps Pay while her metro train enters a tunnel.

Diagram 2 — How a Retry Charges a Buyer Twice

The first payment went through, but the app never heard back. Without an idempotency key, the second tap looks exactly like a brand-new purchase.

Retries aren't a corner case. They come from every direction: the buyer taps twice, the app retries after a timeout, the executor retries a slow PA call, the PA delivers the same webhook three times, and Kafka redelivers events after a consumer restarts. We can't stop retries — and we shouldn't, because retrying is how we avoid losing payments.

Networks only offer two guarantees: at-least-once (keep retrying until you hear back) or at-most-once (never retry). A payment needs both at once. The way to get that is to retry freely and make every retry harmless:

The Rule exactly-once = at-least-once delivery + idempotent processing

Three Layers of Idempotency

An idempotency key at the front door isn't enough. Every hop that can be retried needs its own guard.

Layer 1: An Idempotency Key on the API

+

When Meera taps Pay, the app generates a UUID and sends it as Idempotency-Key. Every retry of that tap reuses it. A new tap after a definite failure gets a new key.

On the server, a unique constraint does the work. The first request inserts the attempt row. A retry hits the constraint, inserts nothing, and gets back the response the first request stored. If the first request is still talking to the PA, there's no stored response yet, so we return 409 with Retry-After: 1 and the app asks again a second later.

SQL -- 1. Claim the key. Only one request can win this insert. INSERT INTO payment_attempts (attempt_id, checkout_id, idempotency_key, method, amount_paise, status) VALUES ('att_9KX2', 'chk_5TQ1', '6f1c2a0e-…', 'UPI_INTENT', 179800, 'CREATED') ON CONFLICT (idempotency_key) DO NOTHING RETURNING attempt_id; -- 2. No row returned? It's a retry. Replay what the first request produced. SELECT attempt_id, status, response FROM payment_attempts WHERE idempotency_key = '6f1c2a0e-…';

Layer 2: Our Reference at the PA, and One Success per Checkout

+

The executor creates the PA order with att_9KX2 as its reference. PAs refuse to create a second order with the same reference, so if the create call times out, the executor can simply send it again.

That still leaves one very Indian problem: two different attempts can both succeed. Meera's first UPI attempt hangs in "pending", so she goes back and pays by card. Ten minutes later, the UPI attempt goes through as well. Each attempt was idempotent on its own — but the checkout has now been paid twice.

A partial unique index lets the database enforce "one success per checkout". When the late UPI success tries to become the second SUCCESS, the update fails. The executor catches that, records the money it did receive, and sends the attempt straight to REFUNDING. Meera gets one order and one automatic refund.

SQL -- At most one successful attempt per checkout, enforced by the database CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_success_per_checkout ON payment_attempts (checkout_id) WHERE status = 'SUCCESS';

Layer 3: Conditional State Transitions

+

Webhooks arrive more than once, and sometimes out of order — a "failed" from an earlier try can land after the "captured". So we never blindly set a status. Every transition is an UPDATE that only succeeds from the states it's allowed to come from.

If it updates zero rows, the event is a duplicate or arrived too late to matter. We return 200 so the PA stops retrying, and do nothing else. The outbox row is only written when the update actually changed something, so downstream services never see a payment succeed twice.

SQL BEGIN; UPDATE payment_attempts SET status = 'SUCCESS', pa_payment_id = 'pay_Hx81', utr = '427815903311', updated_at = now() WHERE attempt_id = 'att_9KX2' AND status IN ('CREATED', 'PENDING'); -- 1 row → first time we hear this: write the outbox row below -- 0 rows → duplicate or stale webhook: ROLLBACK, reply 200, done INSERT INTO payment_outbox (event_type, checkout_id, payload) VALUES ('PAYMENT_SUCCEEDED', 'chk_5TQ1', '{"attempt_id": "att_9KX2", …}'); COMMIT;

Retrying Without Making Things Worse

Idempotency makes retries safe. How we space them out decides whether they're also kind to a system that's already struggling.

Strategy How It Works Where We Use It
Immediate Try again right away Only when the request provably never left, like a refused connection
Fixed interval Same wait every time Rarely — thousands of clients end up retrying in lockstep
Incremental Wait a little longer each time Status checks on a pending UPI attempt
Exponential + jitter Double the wait, add randomness The default: PA API calls and Kafka consumer retries
Cancel Stop and report Terminal errors (invalid UPI ID, insufficient balance) or retries used up

The server can help too. When we're overloaded, we reply 429 or 503 with a Retry-After header, and our own app respects it. On sale night, that single header is what stops a slowdown from turning into a retry storm.

💡 Interview Tip

Saying "idempotency key" is the minimum bar. What stands out is placing a guard at every hop that retries — the API, the PA call, the webhook handler, the Kafka consumers — and then spotting the case none of them cover: two different attempts for the same checkout that both succeed.

07Deep Dive: The Double-Entry Ledger

Idempotency makes sure each rupee moves once. The ledger records every move, so that at any moment we can answer two questions for every rupee: where is it right now, and who does it belong to? Any chartered accountant would recognise the method — double-entry bookkeeping — and it comes down to three rules:

  1. Every movement is a journal with at least two lines. Total debits equal total credits, so the journal sums to zero.
  2. Entries are append-only. A mistake is fixed by posting a reversing journal, never by UPDATE or DELETE.
  3. Balances are derived from entries. A balance you can't explain line by line is a balance you can't trust.

Let's follow Meera's ₹1,798 all the way to the sellers. Her cart had a ₹1,299 kurta from a Jaipur seller and ₹499 earphones from a Surat seller. ApnaBazaar takes 10% commission, and as the e-commerce operator it also deducts TDS under Section 194-O (0.1% of the sale).

Diagram 3 — Where Meera's ₹1,798 Actually Goes

The money takes a week to reach the sellers and passes through two holding accounts on the way. The ledger has to say where every rupee is at each step.

Here are the four journals behind that picture. Each one balances on its own, and together they bring every temporary account back to zero.

Journal Account Debit ₹ Credit ₹
① capture · T+0 pa_receivable 1,798.00
seller_payable:jaipur 1,299.00
seller_payable:surat 499.00
② fees · T+0 seller_payable:jaipur 131.20
seller_payable:surat 50.40
commission_revenue 179.80
tds_payable 1.80
③ settle · T+1 escrow_bank 1,798.00
pa_receivable 1,798.00
④ payout · T+7 seller_payable:jaipur 1,167.80
seller_payable:surat 448.60
escrow_bank 1,616.40

Check the results. pa_receivable goes up by 1,798 and back down by 1,798: zero. The Jaipur seller's payable is 1,299 − 131.20 − 1,167.80: zero. What's left in escrow_bank is exactly ₹181.60 — our commission plus the TDS we owe the government. If any of those didn't come out to zero, something is wrong, and the ledger tells us which journal.

Real-World Note

A real journal ② has a few more lines: GST on the commission, and the GST TCS that marketplaces collect. Cards would also add a gateway fee line (UPI payments carry zero MDR). More lines, same rule — debits equal credits.

Posting a Journal

A journal is posted in one transaction, and its ID is derived from the event that caused it: capture:att_9KX2. If Kafka delivers the same event twice, the second insert into ledger_journals hits the primary key and nothing is posted twice. The ledger service also checks that the lines balance before committing, and a nightly job runs the auditor's favourite query to prove it.

SQL BEGIN; INSERT INTO ledger_journals (journal_id, kind) VALUES ('capture:att_9KX2', 'CAPTURE'); -- replayed event → duplicate journal_id → ROLLBACK, nothing posted twice INSERT INTO ledger_entries (journal_id, account_id, direction, amount_paise) VALUES ('capture:att_9KX2', 'pa_receivable', 'DEBIT', 179800), ('capture:att_9KX2', 'seller_payable:S_JPR_221', 'CREDIT', 129900), ('capture:att_9KX2', 'seller_payable:S_SRT_087', 'CREDIT', 49900); COMMIT; -- Must always return zero rows SELECT journal_id FROM ledger_entries GROUP BY journal_id HAVING SUM(CASE direction WHEN 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END) <> 0;

Where Do Balances Come From?

+

Summing millions of entries every time a seller opens their dashboard would be slow. So the ledger keeps an account_balances table and updates it in the same transaction as the entries, with a version number for optimistic locking. The table is a cache of the entries, not a second source of truth: a nightly job recomputes every balance from the entries and alerts on any difference.

This is the original idea behind a "wallet" service. A seller's balance isn't a number we set — it's the sum of everything that happened to their account, and we can always show them the lines that add up to it.

The Hot Account Problem

+

Look at journal ① again. Every payment debits pa_receivable. On sale night, that's 1,500 updates a second to one balance row, and each one waits for the row lock held by the one before. Seller accounts are spread out, but this system account becomes a single point of contention.

The fix is to split the hot account into sub-accounts — pa_receivable:00 to pa_receivable:15 — and pick one with hash(attempt_id) % 16. The real balance is the sum of the sixteen, which only reports and recon need. Another option is to not keep a running balance for system accounts at all and roll them up every minute instead.

💡 Why Not Just a Balance Column?

A balance column tells you what. A ledger tells you why. When the Jaipur seller asks why they received ₹1,167.80 instead of ₹1,299, support can show the exact lines — commission ₹129.90, TDS ₹1.30 — instead of "the system says so". With auditors, the tax department and 3 lakh sellers looking at your numbers, that difference is everything.

08Deep Dive: UPI Pending & Failed Payments

Meera enters her PIN. Her bank debits ₹1,798. Then nothing happens. Her UPI app shows a spinner, then "Transaction pending". Somewhere between her bank, NPCI and the PA's bank, a response timed out.

UPI is a chain of independent systems, each with its own timeouts. When one of them stalls mid-flight, nobody knows the outcome for a while — sometimes seconds, occasionally much longer. This is where most "money debited, order not placed" complaints come from. The golden rule: never guess.

Diagram 4 — The Life of a Payment Attempt

Statuses only move forward along these arrows. Every transition is a conditional UPDATE, so a duplicate or out-of-order webhook simply changes nothing.

Rules for Pending Payments

  • A timeout is not a failure. If the PA call times out or no webhook arrives, the attempt stays PENDING. Marking it FAILED invites Meera to pay again — and then the first one succeeds.
  • Webhooks first, status checks second. The PA's webhook is the main signal. A background job checks PENDING attempts with increasing gaps. NPCI caps how often a single transaction's status may be checked, so this is a handful of well-spaced checks, not a tight loop.
  • Tell the buyer the truth. The app shows "Payment processing. Please don't pay again — if money was debited, we'll confirm your order or refund it automatically." Meera gets an SMS or WhatsApp message the moment the status is final.
  • Hold the stock, then let go. The checkout holds inventory for 15 minutes. If a success arrives after that, we confirm the order if stock is still there and refund automatically if it isn't.
  • Know what the banks owe the buyer. If a UPI debit ultimately fails, RBI's turnaround rules require the bank to reverse it by T+1, and to pay the customer ₹100 a day in compensation beyond that. We store the UTR and the failure reason so support can tell Meera exactly where her money is.

When Downstream Processing Fails

Consumers fail too: the ledger database is mid-failover, or a new order-service release has a bug. One failing event mustn't block every event behind it, so failures move aside instead of being retried in place.

  • Retry topics. A failed event goes to payment-events.retry.1m, then .retry.10m, then .retry.1h, carrying its attempt count.
  • Dead-letter queue. After the last retry, it lands in payment-events.dlq and pages the on-call engineer. An ops console shows the event and its error, and replays it once the cause is fixed.
  • Replays are safe. Every consumer is idempotent — the ledger by journal_id, the order service by conditional updates — so replaying an event, or processing it out of order, can't double-count anything.

When Things Break

Failure What the Buyer Sees What We Do
Webhook never arrives "Processing…" a little longer Status checks find the result. If even they miss it, the next morning's reconciliation won't.
PA times out on create A short wait Retry with the same attempt_id; the PA dedupes on our reference.
Buyer abandons the UPI app Nothing The attempt expires at the PA and becomes FAILED; stock is released after 15 minutes.

Multi-PA Routing

Large Indian merchants rarely depend on a single payment aggregator. The executor tracks the live success rate for each PA × method × bank combination over the last few minutes and sends each new attempt to the best one. When one PA's UPI success rate drops on sale night, traffic shifts within a minute. Because our attempt_id is the reference everywhere, recon doesn't care which PA handled an attempt.

09Deep Dive: Reconciliation & Seller Payouts

Why the Happy Path Isn't Enough

Everything so far depends on messages arriving: webhooks, Kafka events, API replies. Across crores of payments, some won't. Reconciliation is the safety net that doesn't trust any single message. Every morning, it compares three independent records of the same money.

Diagram 5 — Three-Way Reconciliation, Every Morning

Three independent records of the same money. Almost everything matches; the rest is sorted by whether a machine or a person should fix it.

  • Our ledger says what we believe happened.
  • The PA's settlement file, downloaded each morning, says what the PA processed: every payment with our reference, the bank's UTR, the fee, and which settlement batch it went into.
  • The escrow bank statement says what money actually arrived. One credit per settlement batch, which should equal the batch total minus fees.
What Recon Finds Likely Cause Fix
PA captured it, our ledger has nothing Webhook lost and status checks missed Auto: post the capture, then confirm the order or refund it
We captured it, the PA file doesn't have it Settled in the next batch (cut-off timing) Auto: carry forward a day; alert if still missing on day three
PA fee differs from the contract Wrong pricing applied Manual: finance raises a dispute with the PA
Bank credit lower than the batch Unexpected deduction Manual: finance investigates with the bank
Money in escrow with no reference Unknown Manual: parked in a suspense account until explained

Mismatches fall into three groups: known and automatable, known but needing a person, and unknown. The goal over time is to move cases up that list: every unknown that gets explained becomes a rule, and every manual fix done often enough becomes automatic.

Recon also runs inside our own walls. Every SUCCESS attempt must have a capture journal, and every capture journal must have a confirmed or refunded order. This catches bugs in our own consumers long before a seller notices.

Paying the Sellers

Pay-out is pay-in in reverse — and in some ways riskier, because money that leaves for the wrong account is very hard to get back.

  • When. A seller order's money becomes payable once the return window closes, seven days after delivery. A daily run at 10 AM pays every seller their eligible balance in one transfer.
  • Where. Every seller's bank account is verified at onboarding with a penny drop: we send ₹1 over IMPS and check that the account holder's name the bank returns matches the seller's KYC.
  • Exactly once. Each payout has a payout_id that we send to the bank as the transfer reference, and the bank rejects duplicates. A payout that times out stays PENDING until its UTR appears on the bank statement — never retried blindly.
  • Ledger first. Before calling the bank, we move the money from seller_payable to payout_in_transit. When the bank confirms, it moves on to the escrow. If the transfer fails — say, the account was closed — a reversing journal puts it back and the seller is asked to update their bank details.
Rail Amount Speed Used For
IMPS Up to ₹5 lakh Instant, 24×7 Most daily payouts, penny drops
NEFT Any amount Half-hourly batches, 24×7 Larger payouts that aren't urgent
RTGS ₹2 lakh and above Real time Big sellers after the festive sale
⚠️ Four Eyes on Money Out

Any payout or ledger adjustment made by hand — outside the daily run — needs a maker to create it and a different checker to approve it. Changing a seller's bank account triggers a fresh penny drop and a 24-hour hold on payouts. Most payout fraud starts with exactly that change.

10Deep Dive: Security, Compliance & Resilience

Never Touch a Card Number

Since October 2022, RBI's tokenisation rules have stopped merchants in India from storing card numbers at all. Saved cards live as network tokens issued for our merchant account, useless anywhere else. On top of that, any system that handles raw card data falls under the full weight of PCI DSS. The simplest way to comply with both is to make sure card numbers never reach our servers: the PA's checkout collects them.

  1. Meera chooses Card. The payment service creates an attempt and registers an order at the PA with att_9KX2 as the reference. The PA returns an order token.
  2. The app opens the PA's checkout (an SDK screen or a hosted page) with that token and a return URL.
  3. Meera types her card details into the PA's form — or picks a saved card, which is only a token.
  4. Her bank asks for an OTP. India requires this extra factor for online card payments, and it all happens inside the PA's flow.
  5. The PA redirects her back to our return URL with a payment ID and a signature.
  6. Separately, the PA's signed webhook tells our backend the result, and that's what moves the attempt to SUCCESS.
⚠️ Never Trust the Redirect

The redirect in step 5 passes through the buyer's browser, so it can be lost, replayed or forged. Use it only to show a nicer screen sooner. The order is confirmed by the server-to-server webhook (or a status check), after verifying its signature.

Threats and Defences

Threat Defence
Eavesdropping TLS everywhere; mTLS between internal services
Forged or tampered webhooks HMAC signature checked first; timestamps reject old replays
Tampered amounts The server recomputes every total from the cart
Card theft No card numbers stored — tokens only
Bots and DDoS on sale night WAF, per-user and per-device rate limits at the gateway
Fraud and stolen accounts Risk engine: velocity, device fingerprint, new-account and address checks
Insider misuse Least privilege, maker-checker, append-only ledger with full audit trail
Data loss Synchronous standby, point-in-time backups, a second region

Keep Payment Data in India

RBI's data-localisation rule requires payment system data to be stored only in India. So the primary region is Mumbai, disaster recovery is Hyderabad, and that includes backups, logs and analytics copies. Any tool hosted abroad — error tracking, log search — only ever sees masked data.

Never Lose a Committed Payment

Replication choices matter more here than almost anywhere else. If the database acknowledges Meera's payment and then loses it in a failover, she's been charged for an order that doesn't exist.

Setup If the Primary Dies Verdict
Primary + async replicas The last few seconds of commits can vanish ○ Not for payment state
Primary + synchronous standby in another zone No acknowledged commit is lost ● Our choice in Mumbai
Distributed SQL (Raft-based) Every write is on a majority before it's acknowledged ● When one primary is outgrown

Payment status is always read from the primary. If the app read from a lagging replica, Meera could see PENDING a second after her payment succeeded — and pay again. Replicas serve dashboards and analytics only. Hyderabad gets an async copy, so a full regional failover may lose a few seconds of writes — and the next reconciliation finds exactly those payments in the PA's file.

Getting Ready for Sale Night

  • Tell the PAs. Share the expected peak with both PAs weeks ahead; they scale their side too.
  • Pre-scale and pre-warm. Stateless services are scaled up before midnight, and database connection pools are warmed.
  • Queue at the door. A virtual waiting room at the gateway admits buyers at a rate checkout can handle. It's better to wait 30 seconds than to see an error.
  • Shard when needed. 2,000 attempts a second at ~10 writes each is about 20,000 writes a second. If that's too close to one primary's comfort zone, split the payments database by hash(checkout_id) — every query about one checkout stays on one shard.
  • Batch the ledger. The ledger consumer posts 100 journals per transaction, turning ~12,000 row inserts a second into a little over a hundred commits.

💡 Final Thought

Calling a payment aggregator is the easy 5% of a payment system. The other 95% is answering one question with certainty, at any moment, for every rupee: where is it, and whose is it? Idempotency makes sure each rupee moves once, the ledger records every move, and reconciliation proves the books match the bank — even at midnight on the first night of the festive sale.
